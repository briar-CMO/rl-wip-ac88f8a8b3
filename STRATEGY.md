# Marketing Mentor — Strategy Doc

A living strategy document. Each book gets distilled into (1) what the evidence actually says, (2) the operating principles we take from it, and (3) how we apply it to our brands — starting with **Two Dudes** and **The Whitening Co**, then possibly the wider client roster. The goal is not book summaries; it's a playbook we test against our own client data to figure out what good looks like.

**How to use this doc:** when making a strategy call (budget split, audience, creative brief, retention investment), check it against the principles here. When our data contradicts a principle, write that down in the brand section — the doc earns its keep by being argued with.

## Book 1 · How Brands Grow — Byron Sharp (2010)

Sharp's core move is replacing marketing folklore with empirical "law-like patterns" from decades of Ehrenberg-Bass buyer data across categories and countries. The headline: **brands grow by acquiring more buyers (penetration), not by making existing buyers more loyal.** Loyalty is real but it's largely a *function of size* — you can't meaningfully out-loyalty your market share.

### The laws that matter most for us

**1. Double Jeopardy.**
Small brands get hit twice: fewer buyers, AND those buyers are slightly less loyal. Big brands enjoy slightly higher loyalty *because* they're big, not the other way round. Implication: you cannot grow a small brand by deepening loyalty — loyalty metrics rise as a *consequence* of penetration growth.

**2. Retention Double Jeopardy.**
All brands lose customers in proportion to their size. Churn is largely not controllable beyond category norms — "leaky bucket" panic is usually misreading a law of nature as a strategy failure. Defection reduction is a weak growth lever; acquisition is the strong one.

**3. The Pareto law is ~50/20, not 80/20.**
Heavy buyers matter, but roughly half of sales come from light and occasional buyers. Light buyers are the growth engine — and they're exactly the people loyalty programs, email lists, and retargeting *don't* reach. If our media only talks to people who already buy, we're fishing in the small half of the pond.

**4. Buyer moderation (regression to the mean).**
This period's heavy buyers buy less next period; light buyers buy more. Don't build strategy on a snapshot of "our best customers" — they were partly a statistical accident.

**5. Duplication of Purchase law.**
Our customers are also our competitors' customers, in proportion to competitor size. There is no walled-garden tribe of brand loyalists. We share buyers with everyone in the category — so we compete for *category buyers*, not for a special segment.

**6. Attitudes follow behaviour.**
People think and say more positive things about brands they already use. Attitude/brand-love metrics mostly measure past purchasing, not future growth. Don't chase "brand evangelists" as a growth strategy — evangelism is what usage looks like from the inside.

**7. Mental availability.**
Growth comes from being *thought of* by more people in more buying situations (category entry points), not from being loved more deeply by a few. Salience beats persuasion. Fame beats differentiation.

**8. Physical availability.**
Be easy to find and easy to buy, everywhere the category buyer shops. For DTC that means: distribution breadth (retail, marketplaces, stockists), site findability, checkout friction, delivery speed, and being present when the need occurs.

**9. Distinctiveness beats differentiation.**
Buyers barely notice "meaningful differences" — they notice *distinctive assets*: colours, characters, taglines, packaging shapes, tone. Build and ruthlessly protect brand codes so the light buyer instantly knows it's us. Consistency compounds; rebrands and creative reinvention squander it.

**10. Reach over frequency; continuity over bursts.**
Advertising works mostly by refreshing memory structures in as many category buyers as possible. Reach beats loading frequency on the converted. Continuous presence beats big bursts followed by silence — memories decay, and buyers enter the category constantly.

**11. Price promotions don't recruit.**
Discounts produce sharp temporary blips bought almost entirely by *existing* buyers stocking up or switching momentarily. They train price sensitivity and buy no lasting penetration. Use promos deliberately (clearance, trial mechanics), never as a growth strategy.

**12. Loyalty programs are weak growth levers.**
They skew to heavy buyers who'd buy anyway. Fine as margin/retention hygiene; not where growth budget lives.

### The operating principles we take from it

1. **Acquisition-first budgeting.** The default split leans to new-customer reach. Retention spend must justify itself as margin protection, not claim growth credit.
2. **Sophisticated mass marketing.** Target the whole category buyer base, lightly segmented at most. Stop shrinking audiences in pursuit of "quality" — the light/non-buyer is the growth market.
3. **Buy reach, show up always.** Media plans judged on unique category buyers reached per month, with always-on presence over bursts.
4. **Build the brand codes.** Define each brand's distinctive assets, audit creative against them, and stop changing them.
5. **Cover the category entry points.** Map the situations where someone enters the category (the *when/where/why* of buying) and make sure creative and SEO/search cover them — not just the brand's favourite story.
6. **Make it easy to buy.** Physical availability audit: stockists, marketplaces, site speed, search presence, subscription mechanics, shipping.
7. **Read loyalty metrics as outputs, not levers.** Repeat rate and LTV rise with penetration; report them, don't chase them.

### Honest watch-outs (where to hold it loosely)

- Sharp's data is mostly FMCG/repertoire categories. **Consumables with genuine repeat cycles (teeth whitening refills, skincare replenishment) still have real retention economics** — LTV funds the CAC that buys penetration. The resolution: retention is a *unit-economics* question, acquisition is the *growth* question. Don't confuse the two budgets.
- Small DTC brands can't buy TV-scale reach; "reach" for us means the cheapest broad awareness available (Meta broad targeting, creators, PR, retail presence), not an excuse for unfocused spend.
- Double Jeopardy says don't expect outsized loyalty — it doesn't say tolerate a broken post-purchase experience. Fix leaks that are *below* category-norm retention; don't invest to be above it.

## Applying it: the data dig

Before we declare "what good looks like", pull the actuals. Per brand, per trailing 12 months (and by month):

**Penetration & buyer base**
- Total unique customers; new vs returning customer count and revenue split
- New-customer revenue % of total (Sharp says this should be the majority for a growing small brand)
- Buyer frequency distribution: what % bought once, twice, 3+ times (expect a heavy skew to once — that's normal, not a crisis)
- Our Pareto: what % of revenue comes from the top 20% of buyers (expect ~50-60%, not 80%)

**Acquisition efficiency**
- Blended CAC (all marketing spend ÷ new customers) and paid CAC, by month
- New-customer share of paid conversions by channel (is retargeting/email eating budget to reconvert existing buyers?)
- Reach proxies: unique accounts reached per month on Meta, non-brand impression share on Google

**Loyalty as an output (report, don't chase)**
- Repeat rate at 60/90/180 days; time-to-second-purchase
- Category-repeat context: for consumables, actual replenishment cycle vs our repeat curve
- Discount dependency: % of revenue sold on promo; new-buyer % of promo orders (Sharp predicts it's mostly existing buyers)

**Availability**
- Physical: stockist/marketplace coverage, share of category shelf (retail brands), site conversion friction
- Mental: brand search volume trend vs category search volume; aided awareness if we ever run a survey; distinctive-asset consistency audit of the last 90 days of creative

Once pulled, each brand section below gets: baseline numbers → the Sharp-predicted pattern vs what we actually see → 2-3 strategy bets for the next quarter → "what good looks like" targets.

## Two Dudes

*Category: men's skincare (NZ). Category buyer: broader than current buyers — every man who washes his face, plus gift buyers. Likely long purchase cycles and a huge light/non-buyer pool — classic penetration territory.*

- **Baseline data:** _to pull — see data dig list above_

- **Distinctive assets (to audit):** what are the non-negotiable brand codes — name, tone, pack design, colour? Are the last 90 days of ads instantly recognisable as Two Dudes with the logo covered?
- **Sharp-shaped hypotheses to test against the data:**
  - Most revenue growth in any good month came from new customers, not increased frequency
  - Retargeting and email look efficient but skew heavily to existing buyers
  - Promo periods spike revenue with below-average new-buyer share

## The Whitening Co

*Category: teeth whitening (NZ). Split economics: kits are an acquisition product; refills/consumables carry genuine replenishment retention. Growth still comes from penetration — but retention here is real unit economics, not vanity loyalty.*

- **Baseline data:** _to pull — see data dig list above_
- **Category entry points (draft, to validate):** events (weddings, photos, job interviews), New Year / summer resets, post-dentist sticker shock (vs in-chair whitening), coffee/wine guilt, seeing results on someone else / social proof moments
- **Distinctive assets (to audit):** brand codes consistency check, last 90 days of creative
- **Sharp-shaped hypotheses to test against the data:**
  - The buyer base skews one-and-done on kits; refill attach rate is the retention metric that actually matters
  - Growth months correlate with new-customer count, not with repeat-rate improvements
  - Whitening is occasion-driven — always-on presence should beat promo bursts because buyers enter the category unpredictably
- **What good looks like:** _to define after the baseline pull_
